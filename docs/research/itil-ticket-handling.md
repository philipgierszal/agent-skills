# Trim ITIL 4 to six ticket states

A small company (about 30 staff, one owner as sole change authority, AI agents doing the support work) should adopt ITIL 4's *ideas* but not its machinery. Keep four things: a clear split between incident, service request and idea; one triage step using impact x urgency; a six-state ticket lifecycle in which "Resolved" (fix delivered) is separate from "Closed" (requester confirmed or timed out); and KCS-style capture of knowledge while working each ticket. Treat problem management as a "recurring" link plus a known-error note, and treat change enablement as "the owner approves every production change", with a short pre-approved list of routine changes. Drop tiers (L1/L2/L3), a CMDB, a CAB, minute-level SLAs, a major-incident manager role, and KCS's Evolve Loop roles and metrics. Caveat: the official ITIL 4 practice guides are paywalled and were not read; ITIL claims rest on vendor, study-guide and glossary pages, KCS claims on the primary KCS v6 guide. Everything labelled "Inference" is the author's design choice, not a source finding. No source prescribes ticket states, update cadence, or auto-close periods for ITIL 4.

## Three ticket types, and a rule for ideas

**Sourced.** An ITIL 4 incident is "an unplanned interruption to a service, or reduction in the quality of a service" ([open-exam-prep](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/itil-4-foundation/core-practices-i/incident-management)). A service request is a user-initiated request for an action that has been agreed as a normal part of service delivery; failure or degradation is never a request ([BMC](https://dev.blogs.bmc.com/itil-service-request-management/)). Requests are predictable, usually low risk and often automatable ([AXELOS](https://www.axelos.com/case-studies-and-white-papers/service-desk-incident-service-request-management)). A practical triage test: "worked yesterday, not today" is an incident, "did not have it yesterday, needs it today" is a request, and a security dimension overrides the rest; a provisional classification is fine if it is good enough to route ([Giva](https://www.givainc.com/blog/incident-vs-service-request/)). ITIL 4 treats the two as separate practices ([Freshworks](https://www.freshworks.com/explore-it/incident-service-request/)).

**Inference.** I found no ITIL 4 text on feature requests. An idea is not an incident (nothing broke) and not a request (not an agreed catalogue item); it is a proposed change. Close the support ticket with the code "redirected to backlog", link it to a backlog item, and tell the reporter. A request counts as a request only if it is on a short known list (for example, access to a module); unlisted asks default to idea. "Works as designed but surprising" is a how-to request, and a candidate for a knowledge article.

## Six states, impact x urgency, and what each state must record

**Sourced.** ITIL 4 describes activities (log, categorise, prioritise, resolve or escalate, close), not mandatory states ([open-exam-prep](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/itil-4-foundation/core-practices-i/incident-management)). Records should carry category, priority, complete details usable by problem management, progress communications, and links to known errors and workarounds (same source). Tools add states such as Waiting for customer ([Atlassian community](https://community.atlassian.com/t5/Jira-Service-Management/Jira-Service-Desk-status-definitions/qaq-p/1518942)). Priority comes from a two-dimensional impact and urgency matrix, not first-come-first-served; ITIL's term is impact, not severity ([IFS](https://blog.ifs.com/?p=26943)). A 3x3 grid mapping to five priorities is the common template, with cell definitions left to the organisation ([IT Process Maps](https://wiki.en.it-processmaps.com/index.php/Checklist_Incident_Priority)). Escalation is either functional (to a specialist) or hierarchic (to management for decisions or resources); ITIL 4 also promotes swarming, where several people work together until the right resolver is clear ([AXELOS](https://www.axelos.com/resource-hub/blog/itil_4_swarming)).

**Inference.** Use these states:

| State | Required to enter | Must be recorded |
|---|---|---|
| New | Ticket filed | Reporter, time, description, where in the ERP, expected vs actual |
| Triaged | Classified in one step | Type, module, impact, urgency, priority, one named owner |
| In progress | Someone is working | Assignee, a note per step, any workaround |
| Waiting for requester | Needs information or confirmation | The exact question, date asked, next reminder date |
| Resolved | Fix or answer delivered | What was done, cause if known, how verified, requester notified |
| Closed | Confirmed or timed out | Closure reason (confirmed, auto-closed no reply, duplicate, redirected, rejected) |

Add one more waiting reason: "Waiting for change", used when the fix exists but is not yet in production. A staging-only fix is not a resolution.

For prioritisation, define impact as many people or a whole process blocked, a few people or a workaround exists, or one person or cosmetic; urgency as cannot work and a deadline today, can wait days, or can wait. Map the grid to High (same working day), Normal (2-3 working days) and Low (backlog). Data loss, wrong financial data and security issues go to High regardless of the grid, mirroring the security override above. Category is the ERP module plus type, with no deep tree. Escalation means: the agent hands off to whoever owns the affected piece, and goes to the owner only for decisions, money, access or approvals. Swarming means a few agents (diagnosis, code, data) work in parallel but one named ticket owner speaks to the requester.

## Problem and change: link, don't build processes

**Sourced.** ITIL 4 defines a problem as "a cause, or potential cause, of one or more incidents" and a known error as "a problem that has been analyzed but not resolved"; a workaround reduces impact when no full fix exists and should state the symptoms and context it applies to ([BMC](https://dev.blogs.bmc.com/itil-problem-management/)). The older v3 definition required a documented workaround as well ([IT Process Maps](https://wiki.en.it-processmaps.com/index.php/Problem_Management)). Problem management has three activities: identification, control and error control (same BMC source). A problem record closes only after the final fix is deployed (IT Process Maps). No source gives a numeric threshold for opening a problem.

For change, ITIL 4 recognises standard (pre-authorised, low risk, well documented), normal (assessed and authorised) and emergency (expedited, documented afterwards) changes. The change authority may be a person, team or rule, and a CAB is optional ([BMC](https://dev.blogs.bmc.com/itil-change-enablement/); [AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/change-enablement-in-the-cloud/change-enablement-in-itil4.html)). Post-implementation review asks whether the change met its objective, followed the plan, needed rollback or caused incidents, and a rollback trigger should be defined before approval ([MangoApps SOP](https://www.mangoapps.com/templates/sop/change-management-itil-sop), via search summary, so moderate confidence). No source covers a sole-owner authority.

**Inference.** Open a problem when the same cause hits twice, an incident was closed on a workaround only, or the cause is unknown after the time-box; link all later duplicates to it and reuse the known-error text as the helpdesk workaround snippet. A one-off bug links the ticket straight to its change. The owner is the change authority for every normal production change, and an emergency change is approved by the owner first and documented afterwards. Standard changes exist only as a short owner-approved catalogue with a written recipe each (for example a translation fix or a configuration toggle already proven on staging). An agent's diff review plus passing tests stands in for peer review but never replaces the owner's approval. A minimal change record holds: linked tickets, why, risk, affected modules, test evidence, rollback steps and trigger, window, approver, outcome. Verify a release by checking a clean build log, repeating the original reproduction on production, smoke-testing adjacent critical flows, and getting the reporter's confirmation. Do a formal review only for failed or rolled-back changes; a weekly ten-minute look at releases and open problems replaces a CAB.

## Talking to the requester: ask once, update on a date, confirm before closing

**Sourced.** ITIL's own site stresses empathy, communication adapted to the user's knowledge level, and that users judge the whole service by how the desk handles problems; it warns users disliked scripted chatbots and recommends humans paired with AI co-pilots ([itil.com](https://www.itil.com/Itil-News-and-Announcements/service-desk-relevance-ai-world)). No source sets a maximum number of clarifying questions; guidance is to use them sparingly ([WhosOn](https://whoson.com/customer-service/clarifying-questions-in-customer-support-a-best-practice-guide/)), number them when several ([GitLab style guide](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/support/workflows/ticketing_style_guide/), search snippet only), and split unrelated concerns into separate tickets ([Jitbit](https://www.jitbit.com/news/2282-customer-service-tips-for-customers---how-to-squeeze-the-best-support-out-of-companies/)). Vendor timings for pending tickets cluster at a reminder after about three inactive business days, a second three days later ([Atlassian](https://support.atlassian.com/jira/kb/implement-automated-issue-inactivity-follow-ups-and-closure-in-jira-service-management-cloud/)), and auto-close three business days after Resolved (same source); ServiceNow defaults to seven business days, and a rejected resolution returns the ticket to In Progress ([ServiceNow](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/it-service-management/incident-management/configure-incident-auto-close.html)). Practitioners advise telling the customer that auto-close has started, using a "no customer response" resolution, keeping urgent tickets manual, and stopping automation when the customer replies ([Jitbit](https://www.jitbit.com/news/5358-best-practices-for-tickets-with-no-customer-response)). Auto-close tells you nothing about whether the fix worked ([ServiceNow community](https://www.servicenow.com/community/itsm-forum/incident-management-lifecycle-before-auto-closure/m-p/839190)). Jira once defaulted Resolve to an internal note, so customers saw "resolved" with no explanation, and Atlassian changed the default to a customer reply ([Atlassian community](https://community.atlassian.com/forums/discussion/2941527/changes-to-transition-screen-comment-behaviour-in-jira-service-management)).

**Inference.** For agents: search the ticket, the ERP and the knowledge base first, then ask at most about three numbered questions in one public comment, preferring closed or multiple-choice with a "something else" option and stating your assumption ("I assume order S01234 - correct?"). Say why you ask and what happens next, then set Waiting for requester so status, not comments, drives reminders. No source tests the benefit of offering answer choices; it is a design bet. While a ticket is open, update on change and otherwise with "still open, next check on <date>". Waiting for requester: remind after 2 working days, again after 2 more, send a closure notice, close after about 3 more (roughly a week) as "no response". On Resolved, send a plain message (what was wrong, what changed, how to verify, reply to reopen) with one yes/no question; remind after 2 working days; auto-close after 5. Never auto-close High-priority tickets, data corrections or tickets blocking work without confirmation or an agent check. Every comment declares public or internal; anything addressed to the requester defaults to public. Internal notes hold diagnosis steps, queries, hypotheses, other people's data, credential references (never values) and hand-off context; public replies hold acknowledgement, questions, next-update date, workaround, resolution, closure notice, with no jargon or blame. Escalate emotionally charged or business-critical tickets to a human. Satisfaction: one thumbs up/down at close; no source supports a better format.

## Knowledge capture: KCS in its smallest useful form

**Sourced.** KCS v6 has eight practices in two loops: the Solve Loop (Capture, Structure, Reuse, Improve) and the Evolve Loop (Process Integration, Content Health, Performance Assessment) ([KCS v6 guide](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/030)). Capture happens in the workflow in the requester's wording; the rule for reuse is "search early, search often"; responders own the articles they use and "flag it or fix it" (same source). An article has Issue, Environment, Resolution, optional Cause and optional hidden Internal Resolution; use one structure for all types, keep metadata minimal, and keep customer-specific information in the ticket, not the article ([KCS Technique 5.1](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/040/010/020)). I did not read KCS article states (Technique 5.2).

**Inference.** At closure, an agent searches the knowledge base; if an article exists it links it to the ticket and fixes it if wrong; if not it drafts one from the ticket, in the requester's words, with no personal data. Draft versus published is the only state, and a human publishes. Linking an article to a ticket is the reuse signal. Skip the Evolve Loop's roles, licensing and metrics; review the most-reused and most-flagged articles monthly. Known-error text from problem records is just an article tagged as a workaround.

## What to drop

**Inference, consistent with the sources' own scaling remarks.** Drop: tier labels and per-tier escalation timers; a CMDB (use "ERP module" as the one field); a separate known-error database; minute-level SLAs; separate Categorised, Prioritised and Assigned states; a CAB, an emergency CAB, a change manager and RFC forms separate from the ticket; a problem manager and proactive trend board; a formal major-incident process (replace with "alert the owner"); and the KCS Evolve Loop. The sources do not say what a 30-person company may skip; ITIL 4 explicitly allows lightweight or automated authority, which is the licence for all of this.

## Conclusion

The central design decision is where "done" sits. ITIL separates restoring service from fixing the cause; for an agent-staffed desk with one human approver, the safer rule is that a ticket is resolved only when the requester can work, which for fixes means the change is in production and checked, and closed only after confirmation or a quiet timeout. That one rule ties together states, change links, reminders and auto-close.

The evidence is thinner than the practice suggests: no primary ITIL text on states, cadence, auto-close days or feature requests was available, so the numbers here (2 days, 5 days, three questions) sit inside the vendor range but are choices, to be tuned from closure-reason data after a month.
